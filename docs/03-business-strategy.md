# JC Coaching — business recommendations

Written 2026-09-13 after reading the audit of the old Wix site (`docs/01-audit.md`),
the rebuilt site's content (`src/content/`), the ADRs and the launch checklist.
This is not about the site. It is about the business the site sells. Everything
here is a recommendation for John to accept, change or reject. Where a number is
proposed, the reasoning is next to it so he can move the number and keep the
logic.

Audit sections this builds on: §3 (positioning), §4.2 (funnel), §4.4 (pricing),
§5.4 (organizations), §7.4 (blog), §11 (measurement).

---

## 0. The short version

1. **Raise the single session to $195 and show the package as the discount.**
   Today the package costs more per session than a single. Nobody should be
   asked to commit to a worse deal.
2. **Build a "keep going" price.** Real clients stayed 3, 3, 4, 5 and 6 months.
   The 3-month package ends exactly when the average client is still working.
   A month-to-month continuation is the easiest revenue on the table.
3. **Lean into organizations, and give them a number.** One workshop pays what
   ten biweekly clients pay in a month. "Scoped per engagement" stops an HR buyer
   from getting budget approved. Publish a starting price.
4. **Sell the Pivot to employers as outplacement.** It is already the best
   productized offer. Companies that lay people off pay for exactly this, and
   John spent 12 years on that side of the table.
5. **LinkedIn is the channel. Everything else is secondary.** Both buyers are
   there. There are 65 blog posts to repurpose. No paid ads yet except a small
   local Google Search test.
6. **Take payment at booking for singles and offer three instalments on
   packages.** Stop invoicing by hand.
7. **Ask for reimbursement out loud.** Many employers cover coaching from an L&D
   budget. A client who is not paying is not price sensitive. Make the
   employer-pays path a feature, not an FAQ.

## 1. The revenue math, plainly

Current prices, per active client per month:

| Offer | Price | Sessions | Per session | Per client per month |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single 60 min | $145 | 1 | $145 | — |
| Single 90 min | $200 | 1 | $200 | — |
| 3-month biweekly | $1,000 | 6 | $167 | $333 |
| 3-month weekly | $2,000 | 12 | $167 | $667 |
| Purposeful Pivot | $750 | 4 + 2 reviews + mock interview | ~$107 per touch | — |

What that means at scale:

- Fifteen biweekly clients is 30 sessions a month and about $5,000.
- Fifteen weekly clients is 60 sessions a month and about $10,000. That is a
  full calendar of emotionally heavy work with no room for writing, sales or
  workshops.
- One org workshop, priced where the market prices it ($2,500 to $6,000 for a
  half-day with a credentialed facilitator), is worth 8 to 18 biweekly
  client-months from one conversation.

The business does not get to a comfortable income on individual coaching at
these prices. It gets there with higher individual prices, longer engagements,
and org work. All three are below.

## 2. Pricing and packaging: individuals

### 2.1 Fix the per-session inversion (audit §4.4)

Take Option A and a piece of Option B together.

| Offer | Now | Proposed | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single 60 min | $145 | **$195** | An ICF ACC coach with 12 years of HR is priced below market at $145. At $195 the 12-session package saves $340 and includes support between sessions. The single becomes the "try it" tier, which is what it is for. |
| Single 90 min | $200 | **$275** or retire | Keep only if people actually buy it. If fewer than one in five singles is a 90, fold it into the 60. |
| 3-month biweekly | $1,000 | **$1,000** (hold) | 6 × $195 = $1,170 singly. Now a visible discount plus support. |
| 3-month weekly | $2,000 | **$2,000** (hold) | 12 × $195 = $2,340 singly. Same. |
| Purposeful Pivot | $750 | **$950 to $1,200** | Seven touchpoints for $750 is the cheapest thing John sells per hour and the one with the clearest client ROI (a new job). Career-change packages from credentialed coaches sit at $1,000 to $2,500. |

Do not present the 3-month partnership as "From $1,000". That hides the $2,000
tier and makes the biweekly option the default. Show both cadences on the card.
Better still, show the monthly figure: "$333 a month for three months" reads as
a subscription, not a lump sum.

### 2.2 Add a continuation price

The five individual testimonials cite engagements of 3, 3, 4, 5 and 6 months.
Average is over four. The product ends at three. That means every fourth-month
client is either being re-sold a full package they did not want, or drifting on
an informal arrangement.

Add a simple line under the partnership:

> **Keep going.** After your three months, continue month to month at $325
> (biweekly) or $650 (weekly). Cancel any time.

Slightly under the package per-session rate, so staying costs less than
starting. Finding a new client costs far more than that discount. What matters
most is that the path exists and John does not have to negotiate it at the
review session. The review step (step 5 on the Coaching page) is exactly where
he says "here is what continuing looks like." `docs/04-growth-plan.md` §4
covers the arcs and the maintenance tier that sit around this.

### 2.3 Payment plans

Publish them. The FAQ currently says "ask me on the intro call", which reads as
"there is no policy". Proposed:

- Pay in full, or three monthly payments.
- Instalments carry a small premium (3 × $700 for the weekly tier, 3 × $350 for
  biweekly). Or no premium if John would rather. Either way, write it down.
- Retention pricing (continuation, six-month prepay, maintenance tier) is in
  `docs/04-growth-plan.md` §4.2.

### 2.4 Employer reimbursement as a headline

FAQ 6 says the right thing in the wrong place. Turn it into a feature:

- A line on the Coaching page near the prices: "Many clients get this covered by
  their company's learning budget. Here is how to ask." linking to a short
  page.
- That page carries a one-paragraph engagement description an employee can paste
  into an email to their manager or HR, plus "John invoices your employer
  directly."
- Have a W-9, a professional liability certificate and a standard coaching
  agreement with an employer-paid variant ready. Procurement asks for all three
  and delays kill deals.

When the employer pays, price sensitivity disappears and the client shows up
more reliably. This one page is worth more than any discount.

### 2.5 What not to add yet

- A group cohort ("burnout recovery for high-achievers", 6 weeks, 6 to 8 people,
  $600 to $900 each) is a good second product. It lowers the entry price,
  improves John's hourly rate, and burnout responds well to peers ("collective
  burnout" is already in his language). But it needs a list to launch to. Build
  the email list first (§5), then run one.
- A digital course. Not now. The journals already cover the "product" slot and
  courses are a marketing job, not a coaching job.

## 3. Pricing and packaging: organizations

### 3.1 Keep the org side, and lean in

The checklist asks whether `/for-organizations` should stay. Yes, and it should
get more attention than it has, because:

- The four org testimonials are the strongest quotes John has. "The impact John
  had in one session on this company as a whole" and "dozens of managers" are
  numbers a buyer can picture.
- 12 years in corporate HR is a warm network of exactly the people who buy
  workshops. It is also the credibility no other burnout coach in the market
  has: he has sat in the budget meeting.
- One workshop funds a month. Workshop attendees also book intro calls. Org work
  is both revenue and a funnel for individual clients.
- Burnout, resilience and psychological safety are line items in L&D budgets.
  Budgets for next year get set in Q4. It is September. Pitch now.

### 3.2 Give it a number

"Scoped per engagement" is honest and it is why HR people do not email. A buyer
needs a figure to get a yes from their director before they will book a call.
Productize three tiers with a starting price:

| Tier | Format | Starting price |
|---|---|---|
| **Lunch-and-learn** | 60 to 90 minutes, virtual, up to ~40 people. One of the seven listed topics. | from $2,000 |
| **Workshop series** | Three 90-minute sessions over six weeks, one team or one manager cohort, with a pre-survey and a short readout. | from $6,000 |
| **Partnership** | Ongoing: monthly sessions, 1:1 coaching for a few leaders, advisory time for HR. Scoped. | from $2,500 a month |

The numbers are a starting point. The point is that a range exists. On-site
adds travel. The seven topics already on the workshops offer become the menu.

### 3.3 The Pivot as outplacement

Companies that lay people off buy outplacement, usually from large firms at
$1,500 to $5,000 per person for a generic programme. John's Purposeful Pivot
(four sessions, two resume reviews, a mock interview) is a better product than
most of those at a lower price, delivered by someone who ran the HR side of
layoffs for years.

Offer it to HR as "Purposeful Pivot for teams": a per-person price around
$1,500, sold in blocks of five. One HR contact with one reduction in force is
five to twenty clients. It also does real good for people John already cares
about.

### 3.4 ERGs are a side door

Employee resource groups have their own programming budgets and are always
looking for speakers, especially around Pride month and mental-health awareness
month (May). John's own story is on his About page. If he is comfortable with
it, LGBTQ+ ERGs are a natural first audience for a lunch-and-learn, and a
lunch-and-learn is how the L&D team meets him. This is his call and it should
be an explicit yes or no, not a drift.

## 4. Messaging: where to lean in

The positioning work in the audit is right. Three sharpenings:

1. **"The burnout coach who spent 12 years in HR."** This is the sentence that
   no competitor can say. For individuals it means he knows how performance
   reviews, leave, PIPs and "let's circle back on your workload" actually work
   from the inside. For orgs it means he speaks their language. Use it in the
   hero subhead, the LinkedIn headline, and every bio. Content angle for the
   blog and LinkedIn: "What HR won't tell you about burnout."
2. **Name the method.** He has a thesis ("the definition of burnout is broken")
   and a way of working (values and strengths, not templates). Give the way of
   working a name and three stages. A named method sells packages, becomes the
   workshop title, and makes podcast pitches easy. It does not need to be
   clever. It needs to be consistent.
3. **Say "Austin" once and mean it.** Austin tech has a burnout problem and a
   coaching budget. "Burnout coach Austin" and "executive coach Austin" are low
   competition. Set up Google Business Profile as a service-area business and
   route the named testimonials there as Google reviews, where names are the
   norm.

## 5. Where to market

Ranked by expected return for the time. Do the top two well before touching the
rest.

### 5.1 LinkedIn (primary)

Both buyers are there and he has a 65-post back catalogue. The plan:

- **Headline** = the positioning line above, not "Coach & Consultant".
- **Three posts a week**, each cut from an existing blog post. The archive
  covers six months at that pace before he writes anything new.
- **Turn Rooted Reflections into a LinkedIn Newsletter** as well as an email
  one. LinkedIn notifies every connection when you launch one, which is the
  cheapest list-building move available.
- **Comment on HR leaders' posts** ten minutes a day. That is where org leads
  come from, not from his own posts.
- Every post ends with the same line and the same link, with UTMs (audit §11),
  so he learns which topics book calls.

### 5.2 Referral partners

Referral traffic converts at 10 to 15 percent; cold traffic at 2 to 5. Build a
short list and have coffee with each:

- **Therapists in Austin.** He refers out when therapy is the right tool. They
  have clients who are stable and stuck, which is a coaching client. Two-way
  referral relationships with three or four therapists is a steady channel.
- **Cornell alumni career services and the Cornell Club of Austin.** Alumni
  coach directories exist and are underused.
- **HR consultants and fractional HR people.** They get asked for burnout help
  and do not deliver it themselves.
- **Local HR bodies:** Austin SHRM, ATD Austin, DisruptHR Austin. A five-minute
  DisruptHR talk titled after a blog post is a direct line to org buyers.

### 5.3 Speaking and podcasts

Pitch HR and career podcasts with the contrarian titles ("Burnout is a liar").
Each appearance is an audience, a backlink and a clip for LinkedIn. One a month
is plenty.

### 5.4 Email

The burnout check is the right lead magnet. Two suggestions:

- Make the check itself the product, not a PDF. Ten yes/no questions answered on
  the page, an instant result band, and the email address exchanged for "the one
  practice to try this week". Completion is far higher than a download, and the
  result band can route: high band to the intro call, low band to the Highlight
  of My Day journal.
- Pick Kit (ConvertKit) as the email provider. The free tier covers the first
  10,000 subscribers, it delivers the practice automatically, and it can run the
  cohort waitlist and a paid newsletter later without switching tools.

Send one email every two weeks: the newest post, one line, one link. Do not
overthink it.

### 5.5 Paid advertising

Not yet, with one exception.

- **Google Search, local, $10 to $15 a day**, on "burnout coach Austin",
  "executive coach Austin", "career coach Austin". High intent, low competition,
  cheap to test for a month. Land it on the Coaching page.
- **No Meta or LinkedIn ads.** Coaching leads from social ads run $50 to $150
  each with low intent, and LinkedIn's minimums make it a B2B tool for much
  bigger tickets. Revisit only if the organic engine is running and the intro
  call converts well.

### 5.6 Instagram and TikTok

Pick one or drop both. The site is right to move the icons to the footer. Short
video of John talking would work for burnout content, but it is a second job.
LinkedIn first. If he enjoys video, TikTok clips of the same points cost little
extra. If he does not, stop.

## 6. Payments and the booking flow

Today everything goes through Calendly and, presumably, invoices. Proposed:

| Step | Tool | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Intro call | Calendly, free event, one intake question ("What is going on?") | The question raises show-up rates and screens for therapy-first cases before the call. |
| Single session | Calendly paid event via Stripe, payment at booking | No invoicing, no no-shows. Calendly supports this natively. |
| Packages | Stripe Payment Link (pay in full) or three scheduled Stripe invoices (instalments), plus an e-signed coaching agreement | Zero code, links can sit on the site as buttons once the pricing decision lands. |
| Org work | Stripe invoice, net 30, with W-9 and insurance certificate on file | Companies pay on their terms; be ready for them. |

If John would rather have one tool, Paperbell or Practice bundle scheduling,
packages with session credits, contracts and instalments for roughly $40 a
month. That is the right move once he has more than ten active clients.

Calendly stays the only vendor named in the site code either way. Payment links
would be added to `src/config/cta.ts` alongside the two events, not scattered.

## 7. Retention and referrals

- **Ask at the review session.** Step 5 on the Coaching page is "A review".
  That is the moment to ask three things: continue month to month, a
  testimonial with first name and role (with a one-line consent form), and
  "who else should be talking to me?" Doing this in the room beats an email a
  month later, which is how nine testimonials ended up anonymous.
- **Send a journal.** A physical copy of the Rest Regimen journal to every new
  partnership client costs about ten dollars and makes a $1,000 purchase feel
  considered. It is also a reminder on their desk.
- **Track four numbers** in a spreadsheet, monthly: intro calls booked, calls
  that became clients, source of each client, and active client-months. Nothing
  else matters until those four move.
