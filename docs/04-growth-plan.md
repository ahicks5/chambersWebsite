# JC Coaching — growth plan

Companion to `docs/03-business-strategy.md`, which covers pricing, packaging,
channels and payments. This one covers the shape of the business: how many
clients, how they are found, why they stay, what good delivery looks like, and
which levers scale past John's hours without burning out the burnout coach.

Prices below use the proposed numbers from 03 (single $195, 3-month biweekly
$1,000, 3-month weekly $2,000). Swap in whatever John decides and the logic
holds.

---

## 1. Pick the business you are building

Three honest versions of this business. The site, the pricing and the marketing
are the same for all three. The difference is how many hours John sells and
which lines he adds.

| | Side practice | Replace a salary | Full practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly revenue | ~$4,000 | ~$10,500 | ~$19,000 |
| Active 1:1 clients | 8 (all biweekly) | 12 (8 biweekly, 4 weekly) | 18 (12 biweekly, 6 weekly) |
| 1:1 revenue | $2,700 | $5,300 | $8,000 |
| Org workshops | 1 a quarter (~$1,000/mo averaged) | 1 a month (~$3,000) | 2 a month (~$6,000) |
| Org retainers | 0 | 1 (~$2,500/mo) | 2 (~$5,000/mo) |
| Singles and Pivots | ~$300/mo | ~$700/mo | ~$1,000/mo |
| Client sessions a week | ~4 | ~9 | ~14 |
| Delivery days a month (workshops) | 0.3 | 1 | 2 |

Two things to notice.

- **1:1 coaching alone never gets past the middle column** without a calendar
  that is all sessions. Org work is what makes the third column possible at a
  humane pace.
- **Fourteen client sessions a week is the ceiling for this kind of work.**
  Burnout coaching is emotionally heavy. Coaches who run 20+ a week of it burn
  out themselves, and that is the one failure mode that ends the business.

Recommendation: aim at the middle column for the next twelve months. It is
reachable with the assets he already has.

## 2. How many clients, and where they come from

### 2.1 The replacement rate

Active clients = new clients a month × average months they stay.

His own testimonials say clients stay 3, 3, 4, 5 and 6 months. Call it 4.2. To
hold 12 active clients:

| Average tenure | New clients needed each month |
|---|---|
| 3 months (package ends, nobody continues) | 4 |
| 4.2 months (today) | 3 |
| 6 months (continuation offer working) | 2 |
| 8 months (continuation plus a maintenance tier) | 1.5 |

This is why §4 (retention) matters as much as marketing. Every extra month of
average tenure is one fewer stranger he has to find, warm up and close.

### 2.2 The funnel, with numbers

Working back from three new clients a month:

| Stage | Rate | Monthly count |
|---|---|---|
| New clients | | 3 |
| Intro calls held | 40% become clients (referral-heavy mix) | 8 |
| Intro calls booked | 85% show up with a reminder and an intake question | 9 |
| Warm site visitors | 5% book (audit §4.1 range: 4 to 8%) | ~180 |

Eight intro calls a month is two a week. That is the number to watch. Everything
in marketing exists to make two calls a week happen, and everything in retention
exists to make two a week enough.

Where the 180 warm visits come from, in order of yield:

1. **Referrals from current and past clients.** Highest close rate, near-zero
   cost. §4.5 makes this systematic.
2. **LinkedIn.** Content plus daily comments on HR leaders' posts.
3. **Workshops.** Every room of 30 managers produces two or three intro calls
   if the last slide says how.
4. **Search.** "burnout coach Austin" and the 65 blog posts. Slow to build,
   compounding once it does.
5. **Referral partners.** Therapists, HR consultants, alumni services.

### 2.3 Org pipeline, with numbers

One workshop a month needs roughly four proposals a month, which needs roughly
eight conversations a month. Two org conversations a week, most of them coffee
with someone from his twelve years in HR.

Keep a list of fifty names from that network. Work through it at two a week. The
ask is not "buy a workshop." The ask is "who on your team is closest to the
edge, and what would help?" The workshop follows.

## 3. Angles to get business that the site does not cover

These are buyer situations, not channels. Each one is a sentence John can say
to a specific person.

| Situation | Buyer | The pitch | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| A high performer is visibly burning out | Their manager or HR | "Replacing them costs six months of salary. Three months of coaching costs $2,000. Do this first." | 3-month partnership, employer paid |
| Someone is coming back from burnout or medical leave | HR | "Most people go back to the same desk and the same pattern. Give them a plan for the first six weeks." | A six-week "return with a plan" package, sold to employers |
| A reduction in force | HR | "Your outplacement vendor runs a webinar. I run four sessions, two resume reviews and a mock interview, and I sat on your side of the table." | Purposeful Pivot for teams |
| New managers, first year | HR or L&D | "Nobody teaches them how to protect their own energy while carrying a team's." | Resilient leadership series |
| A team just went through a bad quarter | The team lead | "Name it before it becomes the culture." | Resilience team training (the "collective burnout" testimonial is this exact case) |
| An ERG needs a speaker | ERG lead | Mental Health Awareness Month (May), Pride (June), World Mental Health Day (October) | Lunch-and-learn |
| A therapist has a stable client who is stuck at work | Therapist | "I take the ones who do not need you any more but still cannot move." | Intro call referral |

The first row is the most important sentence in this document. Coaching as
retention is a pitch HR already understands, it is priced against a number they
already know (replacement cost), and John is the only burnout coach who can say
"I sat on your side of the table."

## 4. Staying longer: retention that is good for the client

Retention in coaching only works if the client keeps getting better. Nothing
here is a lock-in. Everything here is a reason to keep going that the client can
see for themselves.

### 4.1 Structure the engagement as arcs, not a cliff

The 3-month package ends exactly when the average client is still working.
Name what comes after so continuing is a next step, not "more of the same":

| Arc | Length | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| **Stabilize** | Months 1 to 3 | Stop the bleeding. Boundaries, rest, the immediate decision. This is the current package. |
| **Rebuild** | Months 4 to 6 | Change how the work is done, not just how it feels. Career shape, relationships at work, what success is for. |
| **Maintain** | Month 7 on | One session a month plus text support. Catching the slide before it becomes a relapse. |

The review at the end of month three (already step 5 on the Coaching page) is
where John says which arc comes next and why, based on what he has seen.

### 4.2 Price continuing below starting

Acquiring a client is the expensive part. Once they are in, a discount on
staying costs less than finding the next person. So:

- **Continuation, month to month:** $325 biweekly, $650 weekly. Slightly under
  the package per-session rate, cancel any time. Staying costs less than
  starting.
- **6-month partnership, paid up front:** 10% off the two 3-month packages
  back to back. Offer it on the intro call to people who already know they
  want the longer arc.
- **Maintain tier:** $195 a month for one session and text support. This is
  the tail that keeps clients for a year or more, and those clients are the
  referral engine.

This replaces the "small premium" continuation price in 03 §2.2. If John is
ever at capacity with a waitlist, the premium version is the right one. Until
then, the discount is.

### 4.3 Show progress with the tool he already has

The 2-minute burnout check is the lead magnet. Use it inside the engagement
too:

- Every client takes it in the intro call or first session. That is the
  baseline.
- Retake at month three and month six.
- Add two or three goals the client chooses, rated one to ten, monthly.

A client who can see their score move has a reason to continue that nobody has
to sell them. It is also the most honest testimonial material there is ("went
from 8 of 10 to 3 of 10 in four months") and the only kind of outcome an HR
buyer can put in a budget request.

### 4.4 Deliver in a way that is hard to leave

None of this is expensive. All of it is rare.

- **A written plan by session two.** One page, in the client's words, with
  what they are working on and how they will know. Clients with a written plan
  stay; clients without one drift.
- **A five-line note after every session.** What we talked about, what you said
  you would try, when we meet next. Five minutes for John. It is the thing
  clients forward to their partner and the reason they remember the session
  changed something.
- **Between-session support with a stated boundary.** "Text or email any time,
  I reply within one business day." The boundary protects John and makes the
  promise real. Unlimited and undefined support is how coaches burn out.
- **A journal in the mail** at the start of every partnership. About ten
  dollars. It makes $1,000 feel considered and puts his name on their desk.
- **Onboarding in the first two weeks.** Intake questionnaire before session
  one, plan by session two, first check-in text from John between them. Most
  drop-off in coaching happens before session three.
- **Say when it is time to stop.** The "not for" list and the refer-out
  policy build the kind of trust that brings people back a year later. A coach
  who ends engagements when they are done gets more referrals than one who
  stretches them.

### 4.5 Referrals as a system, not a hope

- **Ask at the review session**, in the room, at the moment they are telling
  him what changed. Three asks: continue, a testimonial with first name and
  role, and "who else should be talking to me?"
- **A referral credit both ways.** The referrer and the new client each get a
  free session, or $100 off. It is normal in coaching, allowed under ICF ethics,
  and it turns a warm feeling into a name.
- **Alumni email at three and six months** after an engagement ends. One line
  from John, not a newsletter. "How is it holding?" That email reactivates
  more past clients than any offer.
- **Make the client the hero of the testimonial**, never John. "I stopped
  answering email after seven" is a better quote than "John is a great coach."

## 5. Growing without burning out

The burnout coach burning out is the failure mode that ends the business. Rules,
not intentions:

- **Cap client sessions at fourteen a week.** Above that, open a waitlist
  rather than a slot. A waitlist is also the best pricing signal there is.
- **Raise prices by a rule, not a mood.** When the calendar is 80% booked for
  two months running, new clients pay 10% more. Existing clients keep their
  rate for six months, then move.
- **Protect one day a week** for writing, sales conversations and workshop
  prep. No client sessions on it. That day is where the org pipeline and the
  LinkedIn cadence come from.
- **One week off every quarter**, told to clients in advance. He sells rest.
  He should be seen taking it.
- **Batch the content.** Three LinkedIn posts a week for six months already
  exist in the 65 blog posts. Cut a month of them in one sitting.
- **Do not hire coaches to grow.** An associate model is a different business
  with thin margins and a management job. Scale with price, groups and org
  work first (§6). Revisit only after twelve months of a waitlist.

## 6. Scaling past the hours

In order of leverage. Each one is worth doing only once the one above it is
working.

1. **Price.** Already covered. Immediate, free, and it changes the whole model.
2. **Org workshops.** The same hour, thirty people, ten times the fee. This is
   the first real scaling lever and it also feeds 1:1.
3. **Retainers.** A company buys a block of coaching for its managers, say
   five slots a month at $2,500. Recurring, predictable, invoiced. Two of these
   are a base income that makes everything else optional. Pitch it after a
   workshop lands: "Three of your managers asked for more. Here is what that
   looks like."
4. **A small-group cohort.** Six to eight people, six weeks, $750 each. Roughly
   $5,000 for nine hours of delivery, a lower entry price for people who cannot
   do $1,000, and burnout genuinely responds to peers. Launch it to the email
   list once the list exists. Second and third cohorts fill from the first.
5. **A fourth journal, built from the method.** A burnout recovery journal that
   is also the cohort workbook and the "practice" the burnout check sends. Small
   money, large credibility, and it makes the cohort feel like a programme.
6. **Paid speaking.** Once the LinkedIn newsletter and a few DisruptHR talks
   exist, HR conferences pay $2,500 to $7,500 for a keynote and the room is
   full of buyers. It is the last lever, not the first.

What this looks like at month twelve if it works: twelve 1:1 clients averaging
six months, one workshop a month, one retainer, one cohort a quarter, and the
calendar at about eleven client hours a week. That is the middle column of §1
with room to breathe.

## 7. The numbers to watch

One spreadsheet, updated on the first of the month. Nothing else until these
move.

| Number | Target at month 12 |
|---|---|
| Intro calls held a month | 8 |
| Intro call to client | 40% |
| Active 1:1 clients | 12 |
| Average tenure (client-months ÷ clients ended) | 6 months |
| Share of new clients from referral | 50% |
| Org conversations a month | 8 |
| Workshops delivered a quarter | 3 |
| Email list | 1,000 |
| Revenue by line: 1:1, org, singles and Pivots | tracked monthly |

If intro calls are low, the problem is marketing. If calls are fine and clients
are low, the problem is the call. If clients are fine and tenure is short, the
problem is delivery. Each number points at one section of this document.
